What the four criteria look at

Writing assessment considers the answer’s purpose, organisation, vocabulary and grammar. Task 1 uses Task Achievement; Task 2 uses Task Response. The other three criteria apply to both. These explanations are our own teaching summaries. Refer to the IELTS.org Writing band descriptors for the published assessment framework.

Moving from band 6 towards 7 involves more consistent development, organisation and language control. The suggestions below are practice exercises, with no promised score increase. A single improved sentence does not establish a band for a complete answer.

1. Task Achievement / Task Response

This asks whether your writing does the job the question sets. An Academic report needs a useful picture of the visual information. A letter needs to fulfil its purpose for its recipient. An essay needs an answer supported by reasoning relevant to the prompt.

Common mistakes: listing chart figures without an overview; mentioning a letter bullet point without developing it; answering only part of an essay question; or stating an opinion without explaining why it makes sense.

Practise towards 7: write a short checklist from the prompt before drafting. After writing, point to the sentence or passage that fulfils each requirement. For an essay idea, ask Why? and How would that work? For a report, check that your overview captures the main patterns. For a letter, check that the reader knows what response you want.

2. Coherence and Cohesion

This concerns how readers follow your message and see the relationships between ideas. Paragraph order, sentence order, linking expressions and clear references all contribute. A paragraph break should help the reader understand the structure, rather than simply divide a long block of text.

Common mistakes: adding moreover to an unrelated point; beginning every sentence with a connector; using this when its meaning is unclear; or mixing several unrelated arguments in one paragraph.

Practise towards 7: label each paragraph’s job in the margin. Put its sentences in an order you can explain. Circle pronouns and identify what each refers to. Remove a linking word if the relationship is already clear; replace it if it signals the wrong relationship. Read the result without your notes to see whether the progression still makes sense.

3. Lexical Resource

This concerns the vocabulary available to express your meaning and how suitably you use it. A precise everyday word can communicate more than a grand synonym used in the wrong context. Spelling, word forms and combinations of words also affect how easily the reader understands you.

Common mistakes: repeating vague words such as things; substituting synonyms without checking their meaning; mixing formal and casual language in a letter; or knowing a word but using the wrong noun or verb form.

Practise towards 7: underline vague phrases and name the actual people, actions or effects you mean. Learn useful combinations in context, such as reduce congestion, rather than keeping a list of isolated impressive words. Reuse a new phrase in a different sentence to check that you understand its meaning and grammar.

4. Grammatical Range and Accuracy

This concerns the sentence structures you can use and how well you control them, including punctuation. A longer sentence is useful when it clearly expresses a relationship. Length alone does not show that you can handle a range of structures.

Common mistakes: agreement errors; joining complete sentences with only a comma; switching tense without a reason; or adding clauses until the subject and main verb become difficult to find.

Practise towards 7: keep a short list of recurring errors and check for each separately. Combine sentences to show a real cause, contrast or condition. Then identify the main clause and check its verb. Split an overloaded sentence when the relationship becomes clearer that way.

Worked example: give each edit a purpose

Illustrative draft: Public transport are good, moreover it help people. A grammar repair is Public transport is useful because it helps people. The subject needs is and helps, and because introduces the intended reason more clearly.

For a transport essay, try a more specific version: Frequent bus services can help commuters reach work without driving. This identifies who benefits and how. It still needs supporting explanation in the paragraph. The exercise shows why a grammar correction and an improvement to the argument solve different problems.
